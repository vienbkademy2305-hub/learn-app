THE HUMBLE SHIPPING CONTAINER In 1956, a converted oil tanker called the Ideal X sailed from New Jersey to Houston carrying fifty-eight metal boxes. It was not an impressive sight, but the voyage changed world trade. The man behind it was Malcom McLean, a trucking businessman who had become frustrated by how long it took to load and unload ships. Before the container, goods were loaded one item at a time. Sacks, barrels and boxes of different sizes were carried onto ships by teams of dock workers, a process known as 'break-bulk' shipping. A ship could spend more time in port than at sea, and theft was common. McLean's idea was simple: put the goods into a standard box, and move the whole box from a lorry onto the ship and off again without opening it. The idea was not entirely new — railways and some shipping lines had experimented with boxes before — but McLean was the first to build a complete system around it, including special cranes and ships. The results were dramatic. Loading costs fell from almost six dollars per ton to around sixteen cents, and ships could be turned around in hours rather than days. For the first decade, however, progress was slow. Different companies used boxes of different sizes, so a container from one company could not be carried on another company's ship. It was not until the late 1960s that international standards were agreed, including the size that is still most familiar today: the twenty-foot box, which became the industry's unit of measurement, the 'TEU'. Containers also needed a reason to spread, and the Vietnam War provided one. The US military was struggling to supply its troops, and McLean's company was contracted to carry supplies across the Pacific. On the way back, rather than return empty, the ships stopped in Japan and collected goods for the American market — an early sign of the trade routes that would later dominate the world economy. Today, around 90 per cent of non-bulk cargo travels in containers, and the largest ships can carry more than 24,000 TEU. The change has not been good for everyone. Dock work, which once employed hundreds of thousands of people, is now largely automated, and many old port cities lost their main source of employment as new container ports were built in places with deeper water and more space.

THE FOUR-DAY WEEK A For most of the twentieth century, the five-day, forty-hour working week was treated as normal in industrialised countries. It was itself the result of long campaigns: in the nineteenth century, factory workers often worked six days a week, for ten hours or more a day. Now some employers and researchers are asking whether it is time for another reduction. B The most widely discussed model is not simply shorter hours but a '100-80-100' arrangement: employees receive 100 per cent of their pay for working 80 per cent of their previous time, on condition that they maintain 100 per cent of their output. Supporters argue that much time at work is wasted on unnecessary meetings, interruptions and tasks that add little value, and that a shorter week forces organisations to remove them. C Several large trials have been carried out since 2019. In one of the best known, organised in the United Kingdom in 2022, around sixty companies tried a four-day week for six months. At the end, the great majority chose to continue. Staff reported lower stress and better sleep, and the number of sick days fell. Importantly, most companies said that revenue had stayed roughly the same. D Critics, however, point to problems with how the evidence was collected. The companies in most trials volunteered to take part, so they were likely to be organisations that expected the system to work. Much of the data was based on what employees and managers reported about themselves, rather than on independent measurements. And six months may be too short to show whether the benefits last once the novelty wears off. E There are also types of work for which the model is difficult to apply. In hospitals, schools and shops, a service must be provided for a fixed number of hours, and productivity cannot easily be increased by removing meetings. For such organisations, a four-day week for each employee may simply mean hiring more staff, which raises costs. F Even so, the debate has already changed how many people think about work. Some companies that rejected a full four-day week have introduced 'focus time' with no meetings, or have allowed staff to work compressed hours. Whether or not the four-day week becomes standard, the assumption that productivity depends on hours spent at a desk is being questioned as never before.

THE MYTH OF THE LONE GENIUS A Popular history loves a lone genius. Newton sits under a tree and discovers gravity; Edison works through the night and invents the light bulb. These stories are memorable, and they are not entirely false. But in my view they give a seriously distorted picture of how new ideas actually come about. B Consider the light bulb. Edison did not invent it. More than twenty inventors had produced working electric lamps before him, and in Britain Joseph Swan was developing a very similar design at the same time. What Edison's laboratory achieved was a practical, long-lasting bulb and, just as importantly, a system of power stations and cables to make it useful. That achievement depended on a team of skilled assistants whose names are now almost forgotten. C The pattern is common. Historians of science have recorded hundreds of cases of 'multiple discovery', in which two or more people arrive at the same idea independently and at about the same time. Calculus was developed separately by Newton and Leibniz; the theory of evolution by natural selection occurred to both Darwin and Wallace. Such cases suggest that new ideas become possible when the necessary knowledge and tools are available, rather than appearing from nowhere in the mind of one exceptional person. D Why, then, does the myth survive? Part of the reason is that stories need heroes, and a single name is easier to remember than a network of contributors. Another is that the system of patents and prizes rewards individuals, so there are strong incentives to claim sole credit. I suspect, too, that the myth flatters us: it is pleasant to imagine that one brilliant mind can change the world. E The myth is not harmless. If young people believe that great work is done only by rare geniuses, those who do not see themselves in that way may decide that science or invention is not for them. It may also lead organisations to invest in finding 'stars' rather than in building teams in which people share ideas freely, which is where, I would argue, most progress actually happens. F None of this means that individuals do not matter. Darwin's years of careful observation shaped how his theory was presented and received, and not every member of a team contributes equally. The point is rather that talent works within a particular environment. Recognising this would give us a more accurate history — and perhaps a more productive future.
