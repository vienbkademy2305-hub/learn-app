Yet for most of human history, salt was extremely valuable. The main reason was preservation. Before refrigerators, salt was one of the only ways to keep meat and fish from going bad, which made it essential for long journeys and for surviving the winter. Salt was produced in two main ways. Many Roman structures have survived for two thousand years. The Pantheon in Rome, completed around 125 CE, still has the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. Harbour walls built by the Romans have stood in sea water for centuries, while modern concrete in similar conditions often begins to crack within decades. For a long time, engineers wondered why.

Roman concrete was made from lime, volcanic ash and pieces of rock. The volcanic ash, much of it from the area around the Bay of Naples, reacted with the lime to create an extremely strong material. Roman writers described the recipe, but their descriptions were incomplete, and modern attempts to copy it were not very successful.

A key discovery came from studying the small white lumps of lime that are found throughout Roman concrete. These had long been seen as a sign of poor mixing or low-quality materials. In 2023, however, a team of researchers suggested that the lumps were deliberate. They argued that the Romans mixed in 'quicklime', a very reactive form of lime, which heated the mixture and left behind these lumps.

The lumps, the team believe, give the concrete the ability to repair itself. When a small crack forms and water gets in, the water dissolves some of the lime from the lumps. This then reacts to form a new mineral that fills the crack. To test the idea, the researchers made concrete using the Roman method, deliberately cracked it, and ran water through it. Within two weeks, the cracks had closed, while cracks in a sample made without quicklime did not.

Harbour concrete has a further secret. Sea water, which damages modern concrete, appears to have strengthened Roman harbour walls by encouraging the growth of rare minerals inside them.

Some companies are now experimenting with these ideas. If self-repairing concrete could be produced on a large scale, buildings might last longer and need less maintenance, which would reduce the large carbon emissions produced by making new cement.8:T7e7,THE SECRET OF ROMAN CONCRETE

A  For most of human history, the night sky was dark enough for people to see thousands of stars. Today, about eighty per cent of the world's population lives under skies affected by artificial light, and in many cities the Milky Way has not been visible for generations. Scientists call this light pollution, and satellite images show that it is spreading by around two per cent a year.

B  Light pollution is not only a problem for astronomers. Many animals depend on darkness. Young sea turtles find the sea by moving towards the brightest horizon, which used to be the moonlight over the water; now they often crawl towards roads and hotels instead. Migrating birds can become confused by brightly lit buildings, and millions die each year after flying into them.

C  Insects are affected too. Many species are attracted to street lights, where they circle until they are exhausted or eaten. Researchers in Germany estimated that a single street light can kill an average of 150 insects per summer night. Because insects pollinate plants and feed other animals, their decline can affect whole ecosystems.

D  There may also be effects on human health. Our bodies produce the hormone melatonin when it is dark, and this helps to regulate sleep. Some studies have linked exposure to artificial light at night with poor sleep, and possibly with other health problems, although researchers stress that the evidence is still limited.

E  Unlike many environmental problems, light pollution is relatively easy to reduce. A  Most of us think of memory as a kind of recording: an event happens, it is stored, and later we play it back. Decades of research suggest that this picture is wrong. Memories are rebuilt each time we recall them, and in the process they can change — sometimes a great deal — without our noticing. I find this one of the most unsettling discoveries in psychology.

B  In a classic series of experiments, participants watched a film of a car accident and were then asked how fast the cars were going when they 'hit' or 'smashed into' each other. Those who heard the word 'smashed' gave higher estimates of speed, and a week later they were more likely to remember seeing broken glass, although there had been none. A single word, in other words, was enough to add a detail to their memory.

C  Later studies went further. By using a mixture of true family stories and one invented event, researchers persuaded a significant minority of participants that they had once been lost in a shopping centre as a child. Some described the imaginary event in rich detail. These participants were not lying; they genuinely believed what they remembered.

D  The consequences are most serious in courts of law. Eyewitness evidence is extremely persuasive to juries, yet mistaken identification has played a part in a large proportion of the convictions later overturned by DNA evidence in the United States. Confidence is a poor guide: a witness who is completely certain may still be wrong, especially if they were repeatedly questioned or shown the suspect's photograph before the identity parade.

E  None of this means that memory is generally unreliable. For the everyday purposes of life — remembering where we live, who our friends are, what we did yesterday — it works remarkably well. The problem is that we tend to trust a vivid memory more than it deserves. A memory can feel clear and detailed precisely because we have recalled and rebuilt it many times.

F  Some practical lessons follow. Police forces in several countries have changed how they interview witnesses, avoiding leading questions and recording a witness's confidence at the first identification, when it is most meaningful. For the rest of us, a little humility seems appropriate.
