THE HIDDEN VALUE OF MANGROVES Mangroves are trees and shrubs that grow along tropical and subtropical coastlines, in the zone between high and low tide. Few plants can survive there: the soil is salty, low in oxygen and flooded twice a day. Mangroves cope in a variety of ways. Some species filter salt out at their roots, while others get rid of it through their leaves. Many have roots that grow upwards out of the mud, allowing them to take in air. For a long time, mangrove forests were seen as wasteland. Between 1980 and 2000, roughly a third of the world's mangroves were cleared, mainly for shrimp farms, rice fields and coastal development. Only recently has their true value been widely recognised. The most obvious benefit is protection. Dense mangrove forests reduce the height of waves and slow down storm surges, protecting villages behind them. After the Indian Ocean tsunami of 2004, several studies reported that areas with healthy mangroves suffered less damage than similar areas without them, although researchers have debated how strong this effect was. Mangroves are also extremely important for fisheries. Their roots provide shelter for young fish, crabs and shrimp, and many species that are caught at sea spend the early part of their lives in mangrove forests. Coastal communities in South-East Asia depend on these catches for both food and income. Perhaps the most surprising discovery concerns carbon. Mangroves store large amounts of carbon, not only in their trees but especially in the waterlogged soil beneath them, where dead plant material decays very slowly. Per hectare, a mangrove forest can store several times more carbon than a typical tropical rainforest. When mangroves are cleared, much of this carbon is released. Efforts to restore mangroves have had mixed results. Early projects often planted a single species in large numbers, frequently in places where mangroves had never grown naturally, and many of the young trees died. More successful projects first restore the natural flow of water, for example by removing old walls around abandoned shrimp ponds, and then allow mangroves to return on their own or plant species suited to each part of the shore.

  6. 6. 6 · Most of the carbon in mangrove forests is stored in the soil.

  7. 7. 7 · Vietnam has the largest area of restored mangroves in Asia.

Bài 10 · Trắc nghiệm

READING PASSAGE 2 · Câu 14–18. Đoạn nào (A–F) chứa thông tin sau? Một đoạn có thể được chọn nhiều lần.

THE RETURN OF THE TRAM A In the first half of the twentieth century, trams ran through the streets of hundreds of cities in Europe, North America and Asia. Hà Nội had its own network from 1900 until 1991. Yet in most places they were removed between the 1930s and 1960s, as buses became cheaper and cars more common. Trams came to be seen as old-fashioned and an obstacle to traffic. B Since the 1980s, however, trams — now usually called 'light rail' — have been making a comeback. France has been particularly enthusiastic: Strasbourg reintroduced trams in 1994, and more than twenty French cities have followed. Transport planner Claire Dubois argues that the success of these schemes came from treating the tram as part of a wider plan to redesign the city centre, not simply as a means of transport. C Supporters point to several advantages. Trams can carry far more passengers than buses, produce no exhaust fumes on the street and give a smoother ride. Research by economist Daniel Hughes suggests that property values near new tram stops often rise, which he believes shows that people value permanent rail lines more than bus routes that could be changed at any time. D The main disadvantage is cost. Building a tram line requires moving underground pipes and cables and rebuilding the road, and can cost many times more than an equivalent bus service. Construction can also disrupt businesses for years. Professor Ruth Okoye, who has studied several projects, warns that cities often underestimate both the cost and the time involved, and that some lines have carried far fewer passengers than predicted. E For this reason, some cities have chosen bus rapid transit (BRT) instead: buses that run on their own lanes, with stations where passengers pay before boarding. Okoye points out that BRT can provide many of the benefits of trams at a fraction of the price, as long as cities are willing to give buses truly separate lanes. F The debate is likely to continue, but one thing is clear: the choice depends less on the vehicle than on the political will to give public transport priority over private cars. As Dubois puts it, 'A tram stuck in traffic is no better than a bus.'

Bài 13 · Yes / No / Not Given

READING PASSAGE 3 · Câu 27–32. Câu nhận định có khớp với QUAN ĐIỂM của tác giả không? Chọn YES / NO / NOT GIVEN.

WHY DO WE PUT THINGS OFF? A Almost everyone procrastinates sometimes, delaying a task even though we know the delay will probably make things worse. The common explanation is that procrastinators are lazy or poor at managing their time. I have come to believe that this explanation is, for the most part, mistaken. B A growing body of research suggests that procrastination is mainly a problem of managing emotions, not time. When a task makes us feel anxious, bored or insecure — a difficult report, a tax form, an email we are afraid to send — putting it off provides immediate relief. The relief is real but short-lived, and the task, along with the bad feelings, is still waiting for us later, often larger than before. C This helps to explain why time-management techniques so often fail. A detailed timetable does nothing to reduce the anxiety a task produces; indeed, seeing a long list of unfinished tasks may make the feeling worse. It also explains why procrastination is not related to how busy people are: some of the most chronic procrastinators have plenty of free time. D Interestingly, one of the most effective responses appears to be self-compassion. In a study of university students, those who forgave themselves for procrastinating before their first exam procrastinated less before the next one. Being hard on ourselves, it seems, adds to the negative feelings that caused the delay in the first place. E Other practical strategies target the emotions directly. Making the first step very small — 'open the document and write one sentence' — reduces the feeling of threat. Focusing on how we will feel once a task is finished, rather than on the task itself, can also help. I would add that it is worth noticing which tasks we repeatedly avoid: they often reveal something about our fears, or about work we are doing for the wrong reasons. F None of this means that procrastination is harmless. Chronic procrastination has been linked to stress, poorer health and lower performance. But treating it as a moral failing is unhelpful. Understanding it as an emotional habit — one that can be changed — is both kinder and, the evidence suggests, more effective.

  5. 5. 31 · Self-compassion is more effective for students than for older adults.

  6. 6. 32 · Tasks we avoid repeatedly may tell us something about ourselves.
