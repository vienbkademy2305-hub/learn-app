Researchers estimate that around forty per cent of our daily actions are habits rather than conscious decisions. A useful way to understand a habit is as a loop with three parts. First, there is a cue, something in our environment that triggers the behaviour, such as a time of day or a place. Then comes the routine, the behaviour itself. Finally, there is a reward, which makes the brain more likely to repeat the loop next time. How long does it take to form a new habit? A popular claim says twenty-one days, but this has little scientific basis. In one well-known study, the average was sixty-six days, and it varied enormously between people and between behaviours. Simple habits, like drinking a glass of water at breakfast, formed much faster than complex ones, like exercising every day. Missing a single day, interestingly, made little difference to the final result. What about breaking bad habits? Research suggests that willpower alone is surprisingly ineffective. A better strategy is to change the environment, so that the cue doesn't appear. For example, people who want to use their phones less are more successful if they keep the phone in another room, rather than simply trying to resist it. Another effective approach is to replace the routine while keeping the same cue and reward. Someone who snacks when they feel stressed might go for a short walk instead. Mangroves are trees and shrubs that grow along tropical and subtropical coastlines, in the zone between high and low tide. Few plants can survive there: the soil is salty, low in oxygen and flooded twice a day. Mangroves cope in a variety of ways. Some species filter salt out at their roots, while others get rid of it through their leaves. Many have roots that grow upwards out of the mud, allowing them to take in air.

For a long time, mangrove forests were seen as wasteland. Between 1980 and 2000, roughly a third of the world's mangroves were cleared, mainly for shrimp farms, rice fields and coastal development. Only recently has their true value been widely recognised.

The most obvious benefit is protection. Dense mangrove forests reduce the height of waves and slow down storm surges, protecting villages behind them. After the Indian Ocean tsunami of 2004, several studies reported that areas with healthy mangroves suffered less damage than similar areas without them, although researchers have debated how strong this effect was.

Mangroves are also extremely important for fisheries. Their roots provide shelter for young fish, crabs and shrimp, and many species that are caught at sea spend the early part of their lives in mangrove forests. Coastal communities in South-East Asia depend on these catches for both food and income.

Perhaps the most surprising discovery concerns carbon. Mangroves store large amounts of carbon, not only in their trees but especially in the waterlogged soil beneath them, where dead plant material decays very slowly. Per hectare, a mangrove forest can store several times more carbon than a typical tropical rainforest. When mangroves are cleared, much of this carbon is released.

Efforts to restore mangroves have had mixed results. Early projects often planted a single species in large numbers, frequently in places where mangroves had never grown naturally, and many of the young trees died.
